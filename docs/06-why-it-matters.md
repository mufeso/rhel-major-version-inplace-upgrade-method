# Why this matters beyond the organization that owns the servers

## These are not back-office machines

Servers modernized this way run payment processing, insurance and benefits administration, claims,
health records and public services. The people who depend on them are not the institution's
employees; they are its customers, its members and, for publicly funded programs, the public.

## What happens when the upgrade is deferred

When a major-version upgrade is postponed and a fleet passes end of maintenance, the result is a set
of production systems that no longer receive security fixes for known, published vulnerabilities.
Attackers do not need new techniques against such systems, because the weaknesses are already
documented.

That is why the U.S. Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) lists the use of
unsupported or end-of-life software in critical infrastructure among its "Bad Practices", saying it
"is dangerous and significantly elevates risk to national security, national economic security, and
national public health and safety". It is also why the federal regulators that examine U.S. banks
expect institutions to track each system's end of support (FFIEC IT Examination Handbook).

## Why financial services makes the point clearly

Financial institutions are connected to each other by design. A failure or compromise at one does
not stay there; it reaches counterparties, customers and, where an institution administers
government-sponsored benefits, the people who rely on those payments. The same holds for healthcare
and government systems. Their security is a shared condition, not a private asset.

## What the method changes

Cost savings make this work possible to fund and repeat, but they are a means, not the point. The
point is that systems which would otherwise sit unpatched, in sectors where failure has public
consequences, are kept current, hardened and recoverable instead.
